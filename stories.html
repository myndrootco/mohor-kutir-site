Santiniketan Stories

The abode of peace

Notes on the place around Mohor Kutir — its history, its markets, its music — carried in the terracotta the resort carves on its own walls.

Santiniketan was never meant to be a destination. It began as a single ashram beneath two chatim trees, grew into a university built to argue that East and West could share a classroom, and has since become a UNESCO World Heritage Site without losing its village pace. This is the country Mohor Kutir sits in.

1863–1921

The Abode of Peace

In 1863, Debendranath Tagore leased a stretch of barren land in Birbhum and named it Shantiniketan — the abode of peace. His son Rabindranath, who would later win the Nobel Prize in Literature, opened a small open-air school here in 1901 with five students, built on the old gurukul idea of learning under trees rather than roofs.

That school grew into Visva-Bharati University in 1921, founded to bring Eastern and Western scholarship into the same rooms. In 2023, the campus was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Its alumni include the economist Amartya Sen and the filmmaker Satyajit Ray.

Saturday Afternoons

Sonajhuri Haat

A short drive from Mohor Kutir, on the sandy banks of the Kopai river, a weekly bazaar has run for over two decades. Most of the stalls belong to women, many from Santhal artisan families, selling kantha embroidery, block-printed cotton, bags and jewellery under the trees of the Khoai.

Baul minstrels move through the crowd with their single-stringed ektara, and Santhali dancers perform between the stalls. A smaller Sunday-morning haat — the "bhaya haat" — catches whoever missed Saturday.

Every Season

The Baul Minstrels

The Bauls are wandering singers who belong to no fixed temple or mosque, carrying a mysticism that treats the human body as the only instrument worth tuning. Their songs turn up wherever people gather — at Sonajhuri on a Saturday, at Poush Mela in December — usually unamplified, always unhurried.

Poush, December

Poush Mela

The first small fair at Santiniketan was held in 1894, to mark the anniversary of a prayer hall founded three years earlier. It has grown into a three-to-six-day winter festival at Purbapalli, with some 1,500 stalls of block-printed textiles, terracotta and sweets, and evenings given over to baul, kirtan and kobigan — sung poetry contests older than recorded memory.

Basanta, March

Basanta Utsav

Visva-Bharati's spring festival greets the new season the way Rabindranath Tagore first staged it — students and teachers dressed in yellow and saffron, garlanded, singing and dancing through the campus before colour is thrown, closer to a choreographed welcome than a street celebration.

Underfoot

Red Earth, Old Hands

The lotus reliefs carved into Mohor Kutir's own walls are cut from the same red laterite soil that shaped older temples across Birbhum and Bishnupur. The resort's terracotta is not decoration brought in from elsewhere — it is a craft native to the ground it stands on, worked by hands trained nearby.
